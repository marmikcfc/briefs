A fly may learn direction by changing fast synaptic weights - but a connectome is not yet a learning proof

September 15, 2026 · Target: Peter Wang on X · 69.6K views, 534 likes, 58 reposts, 15 replies at capture

Story summary

Peter Wang and Nico Christie say they ran the fruit-fly connectome and found four hΔ neuron groups that could support navigation through fast synaptic-weight updates rather than changing neural activations. Wang's sharper claim is that this amounts to continual learning of a kind current LLMs do not perform.

It is a strong analogy, not yet a settled biological result. A wiring map reveals possible paths. It does not by itself tell us the live strength, sign, plasticity rule or causal importance of those connections. The interesting question is whether their simulation predicts a real, measurable learning mechanism.

How we got here

Chapter 1: navigation became a tractable circuit

Decades of work on the fly central complex found compass, goal and steering representations. Larry Abbott, Gaby Maimon, Vivek Jayaraman and Barbara Webb are among the researchers Wang credits for turning insect navigation into a model system.

Chapter 2: the full wiring diagram arrived

Whole-brain electron microscopy mapped roughly 130,000 neurons and about 50 million chemical synapses in the adult female fly. A newer Google-led effort mapped the adult male brain and central nervous system. These are structural atlases, not recordings of a working brain.

Chapter 3: AI made the atlas executable

Researchers increasingly instantiate connectomes as graph or spiking models, search them for candidate computations and then ask biology to validate the result. That flips neuroscience from describing known circuits to generating testable circuit hypotheses.

What's new today (monitoring)

  • Wang identifies hΔH, hΔA, hΔI and hΔG as the candidate fast-weight populations.
  • He frames the result as weight-based learning rather than activity-based working memory.
  • The post drew nearly 70,000 views in two hours despite only 15 replies, suggesting high novelty but limited expert scrutiny so far.
  • No linked paper, code, learning curve or wet-lab validation accompanies the post at capture.

What sharp people are saying

Peter Wang · fresh thread
Says the simulation points to fast-weight continual learning in the fly, unlike the fixed deployed weights of current LLMs.
Nico Christie · companion result
Posts a compact candidate update rule involving dopamine and hΔ populations, making the claim more specific but not yet independently interpretable.
Vivek Jayaraman and central-complex collaborators · central-complex connectome
Show that recurrent motifs in this region are suitable for flexible navigation and context-dependent action selection.
Jonathan Pillow and collaborators · connectome-to-effectome work
Warn that a connectome specifies possible synaptic paths, but not how strongly neurons affect one another in vivo; causal perturbation is needed.
Fast-weight language-model researchers · EMNLP fast-weight layers
Show that language models can be designed with test-time fast-weight updates. So “current LLMs don't do this” is directionally true for standard deployed models, not an architectural impossibility.

Where X and Hacker News diverge

The facts, dissected

Structural fact: hΔ neurons sit in a navigation circuit rich in recurrent motifs. Open question: whether the proposed connections actually change at the required speed in a living fly.
Terminology test: neural activity changing over seconds can store working memory; synaptic efficacy changing over trials is a weight update. The claim needs an experiment that distinguishes the two.
LLM comparison: standard inference leaves trained parameters fixed, but in-context state can adapt behavior and experimental fast-weight layers update a recurrent memory. The real contrast is persistent online plasticity, not “learning versus no learning.”
Falsifiable biological test: selectively block plasticity in hΔH/A/I/G while preserving baseline firing. The model predicts intact immediate steering but impaired learning or retention of a changed route.
Falsifiable engineering test: implement the inferred local update rule in a navigation agent, then compare sample efficiency, retention and interference against recurrent-state, in-context and gradient-update baselines.

Both sides, steelmanned and strawmanned

Steelman the breakthrough

An executable connectome can expose local learning rules that evolution already pressure-tested. If the circuit learns quickly without global backpropagation, it may offer a practical recipe for agents that adapt continually without rewriting all parameters.

Steelman the skepticism

Structure underdetermines function. Without measured synaptic dynamics, code, ablations and animal validation, many update rules could fit the same graph. The LLM contrast also ignores in-context and fast-weight research.

Strawman the breakthrough

“We decoded learning from a wiring diagram; scale it and static LLMs are obsolete.” That treats a candidate mechanism as a demonstrated general solution.

Strawman the skepticism

“A connectome cannot prove everything, so simulation teaches nothing.” Its value is precisely in turning a huge atlas into a small perturbation experiment.
