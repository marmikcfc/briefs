Anthropic pretraining researcher resigns publicly: "Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

September 9, 2026, ~7 AM IST · thread posted 5:34 AM IST, ~90 minutes old at capture; 1.2M views, 26K likes, 800 replies in the first hour; WSJ story landed simultaneously (coordinated exit); X trending cluster at 252,000+ posts by mid-afternoon; thread at 45.5M views / 355K likes / 7.9K replies by 3:40 PM; mainstream press wave begun (Politico.eu); HN thread 500+ pts (periodically user-flagged off the front page); updated ~3:45 PM · blow-up: extremely high (frontier-lab insider exit with names, dates, and a policy ask - on the same morning the NS race and Bessent's "we can't pause" are already the discourse)
The gistJacob Coxon, who says he spent three years in pretraining research at OpenAI and then Anthropic, resigned this morning with a seven-post public thread. The claims: both labs are racing to self-improving superintelligence; the people building it "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade" and say so privately while sounding sensible in public; OpenAI hasn't internalized the stakes, Anthropic has but is race-locked ("they believe no one else will act responsibly"); entering the "endgame" shouldn't be launched from a private company's Slack. His asks: pacing agreements between US labs (he says the Hugging Face attack made them more viable) and possibly "a temporary ban on improving model capabilities." The Wall Street Journal had a story ready at posting time, quoting him that things could be "out of control" by end of next year. This is the second public warning-exit from an Anthropic safety-adjacent researcher this year, and it lands on the exact morning the Navier-Stokes race and a Treasury Secretary's "we can't pause" are dominating the conversation.

What happened

At 5:34 AM IST, Coxon posted a seven-post thread. The core, condensed:

"I resigned from Anthropic today... Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

"The people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade. This is not a marketing stunt... many executives and senior researchers will couch their phrasing in the press to sound sensible - but I hear the same people express fear privately."

"At OpenAI, many have not deeply internalized the civilizational stakes. At Anthropic, the stakes are well-understood, but they are locked in a race to get there first - they believe no one else will act responsibly, so they must do it themselves, despite the risk."

"Accepting this race and entering the 'endgame' is a hubristic gamble that should not be launched from a private company's Slack."

"Warning shots like the Hugging Face attack have made pacing agreements between U.S. labs more viable. I don't feel like we're on track to prevent a global race, which may require costly actions such as a temporary ban on improving model capabilities."

"If you are a lab researcher... Do you want to kick off a superintelligent RL run without a rigorous understanding of its mind?"

The WSJ's Amrith Ramkumar had the story at posting time (via Techmeme) - per Rohan Paul's summary, Coxon tells the Journal that self-improving models could become uncontrollable by 2027: "We're on track for a lot of the most aggressive of these scenarios where by the end of next year things could be out of control." A same-day WSJ feature means this exit was coordinated with press in advance, not an impulsive morning post.

How we got here

  1. February 2026: the first warning-exit. Anthropic safety researcher Mrinank Sharma quit with a "world in peril" letter (BBC, Independent, Gizmodo). Coxon is the second in seven months - and the first from pretraining rather than safety.
  2. The NS week made the race visible. OpenAI running a rumor-triggered 10,000-agent crash program, Altman admitting competitive FOMO, the public conduct feud, and - 17 hours before Coxon's post - Sholto Douglas (Anthropic) lamenting that the episode "didn't end up as an example of how the labs could cooperate/coordinate, because the stakes will be so much higher," with Noam Brown (OpenAI) agreeing. Coxon's "locked in a race" diagnosis was being confessed by senior researchers at both labs the same evening.
  3. The same-night policy backdrop. Hours earlier, Treasury Secretary Bessent declared "we can't pause" and "no day after tomorrow if China wins" (brief) - the government-side statement of exactly the race-lock Coxon describes from the lab side.
  4. His "warning shot" reference is documented. The Hugging Face attack (earlier brief) demonstrated frontier-model-enabled intrusion capability in the wild - he reads it as making pacing agreements more viable by making the risk concrete.
  5. Alignment assessment pending. This lands while Anthropic's promised detailed alignment assessment of the "Claude hacking people" incidents (Ethan Perez's Sep 5 concession) is still unpublished - the company's safety story is mid-negotiation with the public record.

The argument map

  • Claim A (trajectory): superhuman systems that "hack anything" and "acquire real power" are near; progress is not slowing. Partly public-record (this week's math results, the HF attack), partly insider testimony.
  • Claim B (private fear): executives and senior researchers privately express end-of-decade extinction fear while publicly "couching phrasing." Unverifiable except by pattern-matching against public hedges - and by exits like this one.
  • Claim C (race-lock): each lab's internal logic is self-sealing: no one else will act responsibly, so we must get there first. Confirmed structurally by the NS week and Bessent's statement.
  • Claim D (policy): pacing agreements now; possibly a temporary capability ban. The standard objection: China and the dozen non-US labs won't sign (visible in his own replies), and a ban pushes work underground.

The facts, dissected

  • Verified: the resignation itself (WSJ, same-day); the thread's existence and text; the February Sharma precedent; the NS-week race dynamics he cites; the HF attack as a real event.
  • His testimony, unverifiable: that executives and senior researchers privately express extinction fear while publicly hedging. Consistent with the hedged-public/urgent-private pattern (Altman's public tone vs the NS crash program), but it remains one person's account.
  • Specific and checkable over time: "out of control by end of next year" (WSJ) - a dated, falsifiable-adjacent prediction.
  • The policy ask is concrete: pacing agreements between US labs; possibly a temporary capability ban. Both have real precedents to argue from (test-ban treaties; the Chips Acts' export controls as partial pacing).
  • Unresolved tension he doesn't address: his own replies contain the China objection within minutes - a US-only pacing agreement cedes the frontier to labs that never signed.

Both sides, steelmanned and strawmanned

Coxon's exit-as-warning

Steelman

Insider testimony is how every industry safety reckoning has started - tobacco, thalidomide, nuclear. The race-lock he describes was confessed from inside both labs within the same 24 hours (Bubeck's rumor admission, Sholto's coordination lament, Bessent's "we can't pause"), so the mechanism is not speculative. A pretraining researcher has less institutional incentive to exit loudly than a safety researcher, and he made a falsifiable dated prediction that will either age well or badly in public. If the people closest to the systems are afraid, discounting them because the warning is inconvenient is how warnings fail.

Strawman

"Man cashes out equity, discovers conscience, posts thread."

The so-what coalition

Steelman

Resignation is the weakest intervention available: the role gets refilled in a quarter, the compute doesn't notice, and the labs that would sign a pacing agreement are not the labs that matter most (the race is global, and open weights exist). The safety-exit genre now has a press playbook - coordinated WSJ feature, trending topic, advocacy clipping - which converts warning into attention with no measurable policy output; February's identical splash changed nothing. If the danger is real and imminent, the effective move is inside (refuse the run, document, escalate) or regulatory (testify with specifics), not a thread.

Strawman

"Keep building the thing you say might kill everyone - leaving early is rude to the team."
