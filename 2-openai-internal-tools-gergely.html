"In an AGI-first world, there will be no internal tooling teams" - OpenAI leadership said it, and Gergely Orosz says they were right

The gistBig companies like Meta employ whole teams whose only job is building internal tools - the dashboards, scripts, and infra that make every other engineer faster. Gergely Orosz (The Pragmatic Engineer, the most-read software-engineering newsletter) reports that ex-Meta hires at OpenAI wanted to build exactly that, and OpenAI leadership refused: in an "AGI-first" world, they said, internal tooling teams won't exist. Orosz's verdict: leadership called it correctly - Codex (OpenAI's coding agent) triggered an explosion of internal tools built by the people who need them, no tooling team required. The twist: an OpenAI insider who works near Codex publicly challenges the story in the replies, saying Codex itself started as exactly that kind of internal tool. The fight underneath: is "everyone builds their own tools with agents" a new org design, or just shadow IT with a GPU budget?

How we got here

  • Meta's internal tooling is industry legend. Developer-productivity teams there are credited with outsized leverage - build one good internal tool and thousands of engineers get faster. It is the canonical "worked great" that the ex-Meta hires wanted to replicate.
  • Codex itself started as an internal tool. Per Tibo (OpenAI, who works near the Codex team), its explicit goal was to be the highest-leverage tool for accelerating infrastructure buildout - which makes it both the proof of the no-tooling-teams thesis and a counter-example to it.
  • The economics only work at OpenAI scale. OpenAI staff effectively run on unlimited tokens. In companies paying real money per token, everyone vibe-coding their own version of the same tool is a cost problem, not a culture win - a caveat Orosz himself accepted in-thread.
  • It lands in a live debate about agent-era org design. Same week as the 7-agents-running-businesses benchmark (bottleneck brief) and the agent-replaces-testers prediction (Primeagen brief): who does the work, and who maintains it, when agents do more of both?

  • Claim (Orosz): OpenAI skipped internal tooling teams, bet on AGI-first self-service, and Codex's internal tools explosion proves the bet right.
  • Support A (outcome): the tools got built anyway - faster, by their own users, with zero staffing cost.
  • Support B (org design): "hyperscalers succeed != AI labs succeed" - the Meta playbook may not transfer.
  • Counter A (insider): Codex itself began as a classic internal-tooling project; the explosion stands on one team's deliberate platform work.
  • Counter B (economics): unlimited tokens hide the real cost; normal companies cannot let everyone vibe-duplicate tools.
  • Counter C (maintenance): who owns, dedupes, and maintains a thousand vibed tools? Tooling teams do. The cost is deferred, not deleted.
  • Counter D (incentives): the token vendor benefits from tool duplication - skepticism warranted on "worked great."

Both sides, steelmanned and strawmanned

No tooling teams needed (Orosz / OpenAI leadership)

Steelman

Internal tooling teams exist to close the gap between what engineers need and what they can build themselves. When an agent closes that gap, the team's reason to exist goes with it. The people with the problem are the best specifiers of the solution, and Codex lets them ship it in an afternoon. Meta's model was right for Meta's era; the AGI-first org routes around the bottleneck instead of staffing it.

Strawman (how critics caricature it)

"A lab with infinite GPU budget watched its researchers one-off their own dashboards and declared the org chart obsolete. The tooling team is dead; long live a thousand unmaintained scripts nobody else can find."

Someone still has to own the tools (the counter)

Steelman

Generation is cheap; ownership is not. A hundred vibed versions of the same tool means a hundred security postures, a hundred maintenance burdens, and no one paged when they break. Even OpenAI's explosion sits on Codex - which was itself built deliberately, as a platform, by people acting like a tooling team. The job doesn't disappear; it moves from writing tools to curating, deduping, and hardening them.

Strawman (how supporters caricature it)

"Protect the priesthood. If a researcher can conjure the tool she needs in an afternoon, a ticket queue and a quarterly roadmap for internal dashboards is a jobs program with standups."
