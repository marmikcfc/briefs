"I'm sorry, you're not going to die from an AI-engineered supervirus" - a guy who actually designs viruses with AI takes apart Noah Smith's doomer fiction

September 10, 2026, ~7:30 AM IST · HN thread 49636906 is front-page NOW, ~1h old, 36 points / 33 comments and climbing · the essay itself is from Aug 30 (front-page-today rule applies: it counts as a fresh engagement target) · blow-up: medium-high (a credentialed counter-doom essay landing in the middle of the Coxon/Hubinger doom cascade week)
The gistNoah Smith - big-name economics opinion writer - published a scenario piece in which a disgruntled teenager in 2029 asks an AI for lethal viruses and kills 90% of humanity, and declared he has "yet to hear an even halfway-convincing argument" against it. Claus Wilke, a computational biologist whose day job is literally developing and evaluating AI systems for protein design, answered from the lab bench: state-of-the-art AI tools today leave PhD students failing for months to design even simple peptide binders, assembling and distributing a virus is its own brutal problem ("you don't just order a working virus from temu.com"), and the biology itself fights you - the lethality-vs-transmissibility tradeoff is why Ebola, rabies, and hantavirus never ended civilization. His confidence: mass-casualty AI-bio attacks are not a worry "for another few decades." The honest weakness he flags himself: Smith's piece is paywalled and Wilke only read the free intro - he is rebutting the scenario and its framing, and says he'll stand corrected if the paid body addresses all this.

The actual argument (what the essay claims)

  1. Design is brutally hard, even with AI. PhD students using state-of-the-art AI software in 2026 spend months or years designing simple peptide binders, and most designs fail, don't express, or are toxic. Smith's teenager solves a problem "thousands of times more complicated" off-screen.
  2. Assembly and distribution are separate walls. Even with a perfect sequence, building and deploying a virus is a rogue-lab problem, not an API call. (He cites an Abi Olvera piece on the assembly difficulty.)
  3. Nature already optimized this tradeoff, and it caps the damage. Extremely lethal viruses (Ebola, rabies, hantavirus) are bad at human-to-human spread; highly transmissible ones aren't killers at civilizational scale. "Lethal + contagious + stealthy" is not a dial you turn - the properties fight each other.
  4. Scale is the question that matters. A fertilizer bomb needs no AI and kills hundreds-to-thousands - tragic, not existential. The claim to rebut is the 90%-of-humanity scenario, and that one is decades away, "quite confidently."
  5. The flagged caveat: he could not read past Smith's paywall. He says so up front and offers to stand corrected - which is also an admission that the rebuttal targets the scenario as presented publicly, not Smith's full argument.

The argument map

  • Smith: AI-designed supervirus killing 90% of humanity is not far-fetched; no halfway-convincing counterargument exists.
  • Wilke: three independent walls (design difficulty, assembly/distribution, the lethality-transmissibility tradeoff) each kill the scenario; the teenager version is fantasy for decades.
  • Metacelsus (HN): the expert's own imagination is not the ceiling - the whole point of the worry is AI finding designs humans wouldn't think of.
  • gerdesj (HN): the realistic vector was never the teenager; it's state/institutional labs, where the constraints Wilke lists are weakest.
  • Open crux: Wilke's arguments bound what TODAY's tools + workflows do; Smith's scenario is explicitly set in 2029. Whether three more years of capability jumps dissolve the walls is what both sides are really disagreeing about.

Key facts, prodded

  • Is Wilke actually credentialed for this? Yes on the design-difficulty claims: he does computational biological design and evaluates AI protein-design tools professionally. His virology is secondary but the tradeoff argument (lethality vs transmissibility) is textbook epidemiology, not a hot take.
  • Does the paywall caveat matter? A lot. He rebuts Smith's framing and intro, openly. If Smith's paid body already addresses design difficulty and the tradeoff, Wilke's piece becomes a rebuttal of a headline. His honesty about it is creditable; it is still a structural weakness.
  • Does "decades away" survive the 2029 setting? That is the soft underbelly: his evidence is current-workflow difficulty; Smith's claim is about capability three years out. Both are extrapolating; only one admits it.
  • Is this anti-doom in general? No - he explicitly allows fertilizer-bomb-scale AI-bio harm as plausible. He is arguing about scale and timeline, not denying AI-bio risk exists.

Both sides, taken seriously

"The supervirus scenario is fiction"

Steelman

Every step of the scenario fails on contact with practice. Protein design with SOTA AI is a months-long failure-fest for experts; sequence-to-weapon needs a rogue lab, not an API; and evolution itself has never produced the lethal-plus-unchecked-transmissible combo, because those properties trade off. Doom scenarios get traction precisely because no practitioner is in the room - now one is, and he says the emperor has no virus. Policy built on this scenario (pause demands, capability bans) is policy built on fan fiction.

Strawman (the lazy version to avoid)

"AI bio risk is fake." Wilke himself concedes smaller-scale AI-assisted attacks are plausible - his claim is about the extinction scenario and its timeline.

"The expert is grading today's exam"

Steelman

Wilke's walls describe 2026 tooling and human workflows. The doom claim is about what a 2029 model can route around: design difficulty collapses if the model is simply much better than the PhD students he cites; assembly has a contract-lab gray market; the tradeoff argument assumes the optimum evolution found is the optimum, full stop. "The expert can't imagine it" is the failure mode the entire worry is about - and he wrote the rebuttal without reading the argument he's rebutting.

Strawman (the lazy version to avoid)

"Experts are always wrong, trust the scenario." Scenario fiction is not evidence either; the burden of the 2029 claim is still Smith's.
