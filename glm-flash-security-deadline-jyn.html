"We have a year to fix security everywhere": the argument that open-weight hacking models just crossed the line

September 8, 2026 on the HN front page (124+ pts, 80+ comments this morning) · candidate source: jyn.dev essay arguing GLM 5.3-flash makes autonomous exploitation available to anyone with savings · blow-up: medium-high (a deadline claim with named benchmarks, simonw saying the deadline is shorter, and a live skeptic camp calling it security-salesman framing)
The gistGLM is a family of open-weight AI models from Z.ai (a Chinese lab) - "open-weight" means anyone can download and run them, and volunteers have released "abliterated" versions with the safety refusals surgically removed (0% refusal on a standard harmful-request test). jyn's essay argues three things are now simultaneously true: these models run on ordinary savings ($6-10K of hardware), they are near-frontier at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities (84.5% on CyberGym, a benchmark of reproducing real past CVEs), and frontier labs' patching programs (Project Glasswing, Daybreak) cannot outrun deployment reality - a patched kernel does nothing for a power grid running Windows Server 2012. Since open weights historically trail the frontier by 3-6 months, the essay's deadline: within about a year, an Astra-level model is downloadable by anyone, willing to hack anything. Simon Willison's reply in the thread: "I don't think we even have a year."

What happened

The essay "we have a year to fix security everywhere" (jyn.dev, dated Sep 4) hit the HN front page this morning (thread, submitted 10:18 AM IST). The core chain:

"Just about anyone can run GLM 5.3-flash if they have a bit of savings, continuously, day and night. Just about anyone can use GLM 5.3-flash for just about any task, including to malicious ends. GLM 5.3-flash is so good at those tasks that human involvement in those tasks can be negligible. As a result, we are now in a world where cybersecurity attacks can be run in a for loop."

The numbers offered: GLM 5.3 scores 84.5% on CyberGym (reproducing real, patched vulnerabilities from public source + CVE description - ahead of GPT-5.6 Sol's 83.6%) and 54.4% on ExploitBench (partial-credit exploitation, where GPT-6 Astra holds 100%). And the policy ask: fund remediation, mandate penetration testing, incentivize airgapping, penalize unexamined security posture - "carrot and stick," with deadlines tied to discovery.

How we got here

  • The offense/defense asymmetry flipped quietly. Frontier labs saw this coming and funded pre-emptive patching (Glasswing, Daybreak) - which the essay notes were then "sold as products," free credits to security orgs notwithstanding. The discovery side industrialized; the deployment side did not.
  • Abliteration matured. Refusal removal used to degrade models noticeably. The essay cites DealignAI's abliterated GLM scoring 0% on HarmBench-320 refusals - the guardrail is now optional, not just weak.
  • Consumer hardware caught up. The M5 Mac Studio (256GB unified memory, ~$9.5K, out Sep 22) is the essay's symbol: near-frontier cyber capability on a prosumer desktop at usable speeds.
  • The insider rumbling predates the essay. On Sep 6, Manish (ManishEarth): "yeah I have access to Daybreak, work with people with access to Mythos and Daybreak Red and it's scary. everyone is scrambling a bit, especially now that the latest GLM is super good too."

The facts, dissected

  • The benchmarks are real but measure reproduction, not discovery: CyberGym scores re-finding known CVEs from public code plus a description. Novel zero-day generation is a harder, less-measured claim - the essay's "0day machines" framing runs ahead of its cited numbers.
  • The "one year" is an extrapolation, not a measurement: GLM's historical 3-6 month frontier lag, extended to "Astra-level by this time next year." Plausible, not established.
  • The refusal-removal claim is the strongest single fact: 0% HarmBench-320 refusals on the abliterated model means willingness is a solved problem for attackers; capability remains the question.
  • The deployment asymmetry is the load-bearing insight and the least contested: finding bugs got cheap; shipping fixes to hospitals, grids, and local governments did not. Even skeptics in the thread accept this leg.
  • Conceded in-essay: abliterated models may be worse at malicious tasks they weren't trained on, and "break this" to working exploit may still need humans. "But those things are temporary" is doing the heavy lifting.

Both sides, steelmanned and strawmanned

The deadline is real (jyn, simonw)

Steelman

Every leg is independently verified: the models reproduce real CVEs at scale, the refusals are gone, the hardware is consumer-priced, and the defense bottleneck is deployment - which no model accelerates. The missing disasters so far prove nothing: exploitation campaigns have a cost-and-motivation ramp, and the lag between capability and incident has always been measured in quarters. The time to fund remediation is before the brownout, and policy cycles are slower than model cycles.

Strawman (how critics caricature it)

"The benchmark says it can re-derive last year's CVEs, therefore the power grid has twelve months. Sign the grant proposal before the vibes wear off."

The deadline is a sales pitch (the skeptics)

Steelman

Capability demos have predicted imminent cyber-apocalypse every quarter for two years, and the body count is still zero - because the hard part was never finding vulnerabilities, it's weaponizing them against defended, heterogeneous, monitored targets, and that's where humans are still load-bearing. The essay's own asymmetry gives the game away: defenders allegedly need careful loops while attackers get a for loop. Meanwhile everyone raising the alarm sells the cure - inference, pentesting credits, or consulting. Patch your stuff, segment your network, and stop funding panic.

Strawman (how supporters caricature it)

"Nothing has happened yet, so nothing will ever happen - the same due diligence that brought you Windows Server 2012 in a substation, now with the confidence of survival bias."
