Google's ad optimizer may be paying for the appearance of growth, then using that fraud as training data

September 12, 2026 · Target: HN thread 136 points · 64 comments after about 3 hours

Story summary

Indie developer Nick Abe spent CA$220 on a Google App campaign and found that 33 of 56 billed installs matched a bot-like pattern. The devices installed an old APK no longer served by Google Play, claimed Play as installer, opened once, spent zero seconds and never returned.

The sharper argument is not simply “bots stole my ad budget.” Google's system optimized for installs, counted view-through installs as conversions, and then sent more traffic to placements that produced them. Fraud became positive feedback for the optimizer.

When a platform gets paid for the metric and controls the measurement, a fake conversion can look like both revenue and evidence that the system works.

How we got here

Chapter 1: app advertising outsourced judgment to the objective

App campaigns automate placement, bidding and creative selection. Advertisers specify an event such as install, and the system seeks the cheapest apparent conversions across Search, Play, YouTube and the display network.

Chapter 2: attribution grew easier to game than intent

Device farms, bots, install hijacking and residential proxies can manufacture the surface signals of acquisition. A zero-second install still satisfies a narrow install objective even though it creates no user value.

Chapter 3: optimization amplified the false signal

Abe says most suspicious installs followed video views rather than clicks. If those views receive conversion credit, a fraudulent placement looks efficient, so automated bidding allocates it more budget.

What's new today (monitoring)

  • The anomaly is unusually concrete: 20 of 21 installs on one day used an obsolete app version unavailable from the Play Store, despite reporting Play as installer.
  • The two-week denominator is visible: 56 billed installs, 33 with the bot-like signature, 7 from untargeted countries and 13 that behaved like people.
  • The fix changes the objective, not the platform: Abe now optimizes for “won a puzzle,” making fraud more expensive than merely opening the app.
  • HN adds long-run testimony: several advertisers describe rising bot sophistication, while others say Google and Meta ads work when campaign economics and targeting are sound.
  • Google's own policy says invalid activity is filtered or credited: the open question is whether an install that passes attribution but creates zero value is classified as invalid quickly enough.

Where X and Hacker News diverge

X's builder discourse treats acquisition and automation as scale. Timeline posts celebrate agent-led outreach, routine automation and headline growth metrics; invalid conversion quality rarely appears in the success story.

HN treats the metric itself as an adversarial surface. The discussion moves from IP exclusions to residential proxies, platform incentives and the uncomfortable fact that every participant can benefit from a flattering number except the buyer seeking real customers.

The facts, dissected

Fact: the author observed 33 suspicious installs among 56 billed installs. Limit: this is one small campaign, not evidence that 60% of Google App installs overall are fraudulent.
Strong anomaly: suspicious devices used an obsolete APK unavailable from Play while claiming Play attribution. Caveat: the public post does not provide raw logs for independent audit.
Claim: the farm benefited when view-through installs improved placement performance. Status: plausible mechanism inferred from campaign behavior, not a confirmed account from Google or the placement operator.
Counterfact: Google has invalid-traffic detection and credits. Open question: whether it catches device-farm installs that mimic legitimate attribution before the bidding model learns from them.
Falsifiable test: randomize campaigns between install optimization and a high-friction in-app event, then compare invalid-version rates, retention, placement concentration and credited traffic. A sharp quality gain at equal spend would implicate objective gaming.

Both sides, steelmanned and strawmanned

Steelman the accusation

Google controls the auction, attribution and fraud filter while revenue rises with billed activity. Even without deliberate tolerance, incentives can favor false positives and automate spend toward fraud faster than advertisers can appeal.

Steelman the platform

Fraudsters use real devices, residential networks and plausible attribution. Perfect filtering would reject real users and destroy reach. One tiny campaign cannot establish systemic complicity, and delayed credits may still correct it.

Strawman the accusation

“Google knowingly sends every ad to bots because all advertising is theft.” The case does not prove intent, prevalence or failure across products.

Strawman the platform

“The algorithm delivered exactly the install metric requested, so the advertiser got what he paid for.” An attributed install with impossible provenance and zero use is not ordinary low-quality demand.
