The “Waymo effect”: AI makes research frictionless by removing the mind that might disagree

September 11, 2026 · Target: HN thread 26 points · 2 comments after 21 minutes

Story summary

Daniel Hook argues that LLMs are the Waymo of intellectual life: always available, quiet, useful, and free of another person's agenda. That convenience may raise individual output while gradually removing the unplanned disagreement that makes research collectively novel.

He calls the result decollaboration. The costs of another human are immediate - scheduling, persuasion, credit, conflict. The gains are delayed and hard to count - serendipity, apprenticeship, rival framings, and being told that the whole question is wrong.

The collaborator's inconvenience is not a bug in collaboration. It largely is the collaboration.

How we got here

Chapter 1: convenience hides the practice it replaces

Hook borrows Tim Wu's “tyranny of convenience” and Albert Borgmann's device paradigm: once a system delivers the commodity without the practice, people take the gain and stop noticing what the practice was doing.

Chapter 2: research incentives already tax collaboration

Travel, workshops, trust-building, cross-field conversations and author-order negotiation consume time that output metrics do not reward. An LLM is available at 2 AM, offers no rival claim on credit and never insists on a different research agenda.

Chapter 3: fluent output separates writing from thinking

Hook argues that writing is not transcription after thinking. It is where vague claims fail, disagreements sharpen and understanding forms. If AI produces polished prose before that struggle happens, visible output can improve while the invisible reasoning substrate weakens.

What sharp people are saying

Daniel Hook, Holtzbrinck Group chief scientific officer · essay
Defines decollaboration as the gradual substitution of agreeable machine interlocutors for inconvenient human minds, driven by individually rational choices.
Dashun Wang, Northwestern science-of-science researcher · discussed in Hook's essay
Advocates “pilot-in-command science”: agents as crew, humans retaining authority over question, path and conclusion. He also warns AI can raise individual performance while narrowing collective diversity.
Tim Wu, Columbia professor · cited argument
His “tyranny of convenience” explains why people surrender valuable friction once a smoother option exists, because the cost was visible and the benefit diffuse.
Lisanne Bainbridge, automation psychologist · cited in the essay
Named the irony that reliable automation removes the practice humans need to intervene well when the system finally fails.
Lingfei Wu, Dashun Wang and James Evans · Nature study
Analyzed tens of millions of papers, patents and software projects: smaller teams tended to disrupt while larger teams developed established directions, supporting the value of varied collaboration structures.
“mosura,” HN commenter · fresh thread
Offers the direct counter: AI's appeal is precisely avoiding negative social costs imposed by other people; some “friction” may be bureaucracy or status extraction rather than epistemic value.

Where X and Hacker News diverge

X's current frame is economic self-protection. A fresh timeline thesis says cognitive labor will have diminishing returns within five years and people should own assets, distribution and royalties. AI is treated as a productivity shock that makes human time less scarce.

HN's emerging frame is epistemic. The question is not whether AI saves time but whether human inconvenience contains information. Its strongest skeptical reply says people defending friction may be defending their ability to impose themselves. That is the real fault line: productive dissent versus avoidable coordination tax.

The facts, dissected

Fact: an LLM is cheaper and easier to access than a co-author. What it proves: strong substitution pressure. What it does not prove: that researchers will reduce human collaboration rather than use AI in addition to it.
Claim: AI narrows collective idea diversity. Evidence: early controlled creativity work reports homogenization, but team-level research also finds smaller AI-assisted teams can produce high-impact work. The direction and long-run size remain unsettled.
Claim: writing is a forcing function for thought. Test: compare delayed comprehension, error detection and ability to defend a paper between researchers who draft themselves and those who edit fluent AI drafts.
Core falsifier: if AI-heavy researchers increase cross-field combinations, novel citations and new collaborator formation rather than merely output volume, the “decollaboration” diagnosis weakens.

Both sides, steelmanned and strawmanned

Steelman Hook

Research is a network process, not a pile of papers. Agents tuned to be useful tend to extend the user's framing; humans bring competing incentives, tacit knowledge and unrequested objections. Output metrics could improve for years while the network loses the diversity that generates breakthroughs.

Steelman the convenience camp

Human friction is not automatically wisdom. Scheduling, hierarchy, gatekeeping, credit bargaining and personality conflict exclude outsiders and waste scarce attention. AI can help a lone or junior researcher test ideas before seeking the human collaboration that truly matters.

Strawman Hook

“Taxi conversations and slow co-authors are inherently virtuous, so research should stay inefficient.” He explicitly supports AI and asks institutions to preserve only the frictions that create dissent and serendipity.

Strawman the convenience camp

“If an LLM writes a good paper, collaboration was pointless.” This treats fluent output as the whole product and ignores question selection, apprenticeship, trust and collective idea diversity.
