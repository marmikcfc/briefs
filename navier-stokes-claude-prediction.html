Why people are arguing about it

This is a falsifiable claim with a clock on it, made in public, one day after the strongest AI-math artifact ever shipped. Everyone in AI-math circles is taking a position right now on two separable questions: is the rumor true, and if it is, does a privately-generated, human-unreadable millennium solution actually help mathematics - Tao argues it can be a net negative. Both are opinion-shaped, and both sides are already manned by named experts.

Backstory

  • The problem. Navier-Stokes global regularity is a Millennium Prize Problem: do smooth solutions to the incompressible fluid equations always exist, or can they blow up in finite time? Per Tao, the emerging consensus is now that the answer is negative - singularities exist - and there is a defined four-step strategy to prove it: design a nearly-self-similar blowup ansatz (a guessed template for what the solution looks like), find a numerical approximate solution with a tiny computable residual, prove the ansatz is stable around it in renormalized coordinates, and verify the residual falls inside the stability threshold. Every step is enormously complicated and they interlock.
  • The AI math ladder. FrontierMath was built to last 5+ years; models 10x'd its state-of-the-art before IMO gold fell (Glazer, who built it, quit refereeing and joined an AI math lab). Yesterday's FLT formalization wrapped Freek Wiedijk's 20-year-old list of 100 formalization challenges - the last one standing.
  • Tao's warning. Tao's September 3 thread argues that pure-math problems are posed less for their answers than for the field-building that happens on the way to them. An autonomous harness that performs the whole iteration internally and publishes only the result "contaminates" the problem as a source of further advances: technically solved, "almost no value added to mathematics."

The details that matter

  1. "Solved" almost certainly means blowup, not regularity. Tao states the emerging consensus is that smooth solutions do NOT always exist, and names the exact four-step construction strategy. So the claim under review is likely a specific finite-time singularity construction, formally verified in Lean.This matters for the take: a negative answer discovered by a harness is a different headline than "AI proves fluids are nice," and most of X will get it wrong.
  2. FLT and Navier-Stokes are different claim types. FLT was autoformalization: the proof existed (Wiles-Taylor-Wiles, via the 1995 Darmon-Diamond-Taylor exposition), and Buzzard says the formalization "adds nothing" mathematically. A Navier-Stokes solution would be discovery: no human proof exists to transcribe.The FLT artifact proves the harness can do flawless, massive transcription. It is weak evidence the harness can find a novel ansatz - Tao's step (a) is where discovery lives.
  3. The Tao tell is real but circumstantial. The thread is strikingly specific about private-harness scenarios, and it predates the public FLT news. But Tao thinks in public about AI and math constantly, and FLT rumors were circulating before the announcement.Glazer's inference is the strongest version available and he himself flags it as a guess. Price it accordingly.
  4. "Before the IPO" is a deadline that protects the prediction. Anthropic's IPO has no date, so the claim can be neither confirmed nor refuted on any near-term clock, and "expert review" of a gigantic Lean artifact could run for months.Specific-sounding, but engineered to be uncheckable until whenever Anthropic chooses to publish. The honest stance is suspended judgment with a demand for the artifact.
  5. Tao's contamination argument is the part that survives either outcome. Whether or not this rumor is true, his point stands: if millennium problems start falling to private harnesses that publish only results, the field loses the insight-generating iteration that made the problems worth posing.This is the angle with a shelf life beyond the rumor cycle - and it has a natural builder-audience translation: process transparency vs result theater.
