"AI handles incidents, engineers lose touch with their systems"

September 5, 2026 · candidate source: HN front page today (105+ pts, 68+ comments, posted by the author ~2h in) · blow-up: medium-high (the deskilling fight is the most relatable AI argument in engineering right now, and X hasn't picked this one up yet - early mover room)

The story

Sylvain Kalache - ex-LinkedIn SRE, Holberton School co-founder, now at incident-response company Rootly - argues that AI incident-response tools ("AI SREs") are about to create a skill vacuum: routine incidents are how responders build intuition for how their systems fail, and when automation takes the routine cases, humans inherit only the hardest, never-seen-before incidents with less practice than ever. His prediction: average MTTR drops, but resolution time for complex incidents shoots up. The fix he proposes, borrowing from aviation: mandatory incident simulators, the way pilots rehearse engine failures they may never see. post · HN thread

Why it invites an opinion

Everyone writing code with agents right now has privately wondered whether they are getting sharper or duller. Kalache names the mechanism (skill decay through lost practice, not lost intelligence) and makes a falsifiable prediction (routine MTTR down, complex MTTR up). The thread immediately split into "I am watching this happen to my team" vs "you are blaming the tool for incompetence."

Key facts, dissected

  1. The Bainbridge citation is real and correctly used. "The Ironies of Automation" (1983) is the canonical human-factors paper: automation removes routine practice while leaving humans responsible for the abnormal cases, so operators paradoxically need MORE skill and training as automation improves.This is not a vibes essay - the mechanism it describes has 40 years of aviation and industrial evidence behind it. The open question is whether software incident response is enough like flying a plane.
  2. The aviation anchor: TransAsia 235 stalled 117 seconds after the first warning because the crew misidentified the failed engine. FAA recurrent training every six months exists precisely because engines fail less than once per 100,000 flight hours.The analogy's strength: rare catastrophic failures demand rehearsed skill. Its weakness (raised in-thread): most software incidents do not threaten lives, so the industry's incentive to fund simulators is much weaker than aviation's.
  3. The author sells the cure. Kalache works at Rootly, which just partnered with Uptime Labs on exactly the incident-simulation product the post prescribes.Disclosed in the post itself, and it does not make the argument wrong - Bainbridge long predates Rootly - but the prescription section is a product pitch and should be read as one.
  4. The thread's own counter-evidence is historical, not technical. Nobody in 68+ comments disputes that practice decays; the dispute is whether AI-caused decay differs from StackOverflow copy-paste, outsourcing, or "engineers who were never in touch with their systems" (practice9).The strongest counter is danielbln's endpoint argument: if models keep improving, the incident AI can't solve is one no human could solve either - in which case the simulator preserves pride, not capability.

Both sides

"Deskilling is real and coming due"

Steelman

Skill is practice, and practice is the routine cases. Remove them and you get danielheath's engineer: functional until the system does something unprecedented, then helpless. Kalache's MTTR split is testable, Bainbridge predicted it in 1983, and aviation already solved this with mandated simulation. The org-level version is worse: OKRs demanding AI utilization (coffeebeqn) mean the deskilling is policy, not accident.

Strawman (what the other side hears)

"Kids these days can't even write a for loop" - the same complaint made about IDEs, StackOverflow, compilers, and every abstraction since assembly, now with a product to sell at the end.

"The tool exposes competence, it doesn't remove it"

Steelman

Copy-paste engineers predate LLMs (wccrawford); the LLM just makes them visible faster. If Claude can find the one-line fix and your team can't, the failure was hiring and incentives, not the tool (ffsm8, throw839948499). And if capability keeps compounding, the incidents AI can't solve will exceed unaided human capacity anyway (danielbln) - so the rational response is better AI supervision and escalation design, not mandatory manual practice for its own sake.

Strawman (what the other side hears)

"Let the machine handle it, skill is a vanity metric" - right up until the SEV0 that the model has never seen, at 3 AM, with the person on call who has spent two years approving diffs.
